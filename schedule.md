## Wednesday, October 28

9:30–10:30 · **Leilani Gilpin**

> *Title:* What to Know About AI
>
> *Abstract:* Artificial intelligence has long been concerned with a
deceptively simple question: How can we make machines solve problems
intelligently? In this talk, I'll introduce some of the foundational
ideas of AI, including search, knowledge representation, and
reasoning, and highlight their connections to familiar mathematical
ideas. We'll explore how machines can navigate large spaces of
possible solutions, represent what they know, and draw conclusions
from that knowledge. Finally, I'll share some reflections on teaching
AI in the age of generative AI, including our ongoing work examining
how generative AI is changing the way students learn, solve problems,
and think critically abou:Wt their own solutions.

2:30–3:30 · **Eoin Mackall**

> *Title:* On Proofs and Progress
>
> *Abstract:* What does experimental mathematics look like? In this talk I give one
possibility by way of example: a program for constructing either a proof of, or a counterexample
to, a stated conjecture through LLM inference. This program allows for distributed use by multiple 
users aiming to prove or refute a given conjecture, and it can run parallel searches simultaneously 
in any one instance. I'll outline the architecture behind this program, my anecdotal experience 
using the program in some instances, and showcase some future directions that I have planned.

2:30–3:30 · **Mohammed Abouzaid**

> *Title:* Measuring AI capabilities in Research Mathematics
>
> *Abstract:* In the past year AI systems have produced a handful of genuine research results in mathematics: a counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture, progress on two of Erdős's problems. What is much less clear is how to measure what these systems can actually do. I will describe First Proof, which tests AI systems against unpublished mathematics (research mathematicians contribute problems with known but unpublished proofs, and the systems' solutions are refereed and graded), and report what successive batches have taught us.
