## Wednesday, October 28

<a id="gilpin"></a>
9:30–10:30 · **Leilani Gilpin**

> *Title:* What to Know About AI
>
> *Abstract:* Artificial intelligence has long been concerned with a
deceptively simple question: How can we make machines solve problems
intelligently? In this talk, I'll introduce some of the foundational
ideas of AI, including search, knowledge representation, and
reasoning, and highlight their connections to familiar mathematical
ideas. We'll explore how machines can navigate large spaces of
possible solutions, represent what they know, and draw conclusions
from that knowledge. Finally, I'll share some reflections on teaching
AI in the age of generative AI, including our ongoing work examining
how generative AI is changing the way students learn, solve problems,
and think critically abou:Wt their own solutions.

<a id="carlsson"></a>
1:30–2:30 · **Gunnar Carlsson**

> *Title:* Generalized Signal Processing for AI
>
> *Abstract:* Signal processing, i.e. data transformation, is a fundamental component of the mathematical study of data in many classical situations, for example radar, sonar, etc.  There  are specialized tool kits utilizing Fourier analysis, Wavelet analysis, etc.  which are critical to applications.  In this talk, we will consider the corresponding problem for AI tools  such as Convolutional Neural Networks and Transformer models for language processing.  We will show by example what can be achieved in improving performance as measured by generalizability, robustness to noise, and in the case of LLMs average perplexity and feature interpretability.  I will argue that methods such as these are key to addressing many of the problems with the technology, including AI safety and computational efficiency.

<a id="mackall"></a>
2:30–3:30 · **Eoin Mackall**

> *Title:* On Proofs and Progress
>
> *Abstract:* What does experimental mathematics look like? In this talk I give one
possibility by way of example: a program for constructing either a proof of, or a counterexample
to, a stated conjecture through LLM inference. This program allows for distributed use by multiple 
users aiming to prove or refute a given conjecture, and it can run parallel searches simultaneously 
in any one instance. I'll outline the architecture behind this program, my anecdotal experience 
using the program in some instances, and showcase some future directions that I have planned.

<a id="trettel"></a>
10:00–11:00 · **Steve Trettel**

> *Title:* Computing the Light Field
>
> *Abstract:* AI coding tools are making projects that once required months of programming practical to undertake. In this talk, we’ll explore one such project from mathematical computer graphics, where the goal is to produce photorealistic images by simulating light. There are two mathematical ingredients: how light travels and scatters through a scene, and how a camera collects it to form an image. Standard path tracing combines these into an unbiased estimator, spending substantial computation to produce an image for a fixed camera.

> We’ll try something more ambitious: compute and store the entire stationary light field, then sample it with a simulated camera, producing photorealistic images in real time as we move through the scene or change focus and aperture. Under standard physical assumptions, this field satisfies an infinite-dimensional fixed-point equation. Computing and storing its solution requires enough GPU and memory-management machinery that I had long wanted to try this project but couldn’t justify the time commitment. Working with coding agents made it possible to hand off much of that implementation and focus on the mathematics itself.

<a id="lichtman"></a>
2:30–3:30 · **Jared Duker Lichtman**

> *Title:* Formalization and the future of mathematics
>
> *Abstract:* We are poised to formalize all of known mathematics in the next year. This is the mathematical equivalent of the Human Genome Project. Autoformalization has scaled 5 orders of magnitude growth over the past year: from hundreds of lines of code to 13 million in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. Rough estimates put tens of billions of lines, just 3 more orders of magnitude, to cover all mathematics textbooks and literature. Beyond verification for itself, this effort serves as the basis for future discovery.

<a id="abouzaid"></a>
2:30–3:30 · **Mohammed Abouzaid**

> *Title:* Measuring AI capabilities in Research Mathematics
>
> *Abstract:* In the past year AI systems have produced a handful of genuine research results in mathematics: a counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture, progress on two of Erdős's problems. What is much less clear is how to measure what these systems can actually do. I will describe First Proof, which tests AI systems against unpublished mathematics (research mathematicians contribute problems with known but unpublished proofs, and the systems' solutions are refereed and graded), and report what successive batches have taught us.
